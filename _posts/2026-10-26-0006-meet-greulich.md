## The short version

Findings has a third model line. **Greulich** estimates bone age from a
child's hand and wrist radiograph: how mature the skeleton looks, in months,
which is what a paediatrician compares against the child's real age when
growth is in question. Nothing has been trained yet. This entry is about what
the model is for, what the radiologist is supposed to see, and why its first
job is to reproduce someone else's published result before anything of mine
gets built — the lesson entry 0005 paid for.

## What changed

### The name

Every other model here is named after a sign you can see in an image.
Greulich is named after a book. In the 1950s William Greulich and Idell Pyle
published an atlas of hand radiographs, one plate per age and sex, and
radiologists still read bone age by holding the patient's film next to those
plates and finding the closest match. The atlas is not a finding. It is the
reference the reader compares against — which is exactly what this model is
supposed to be: a comparison the radiologist can check, not a verdict.

### A different kind of answer

Damoiseau answers yes or no about one finding. Segond answered a dozen yes or
no questions about one knee. Greulich answers a number, in months, and the
error is measured in months too. It is the first regression problem in the
project, and the first one where "how wrong" matters more than "how often
wrong".

The radiologist is meant to see more than a number. The plan for the screen is
the estimate plus four regions of the hand — distal radius and ulna, carpal
bones, metacarpals, phalanges — each with the age it appears to be. When the
regions disagree, the screen says so and points to the one to look at first.
Clicking a region shows the patient's crop between the same crop from a
slightly younger and a slightly older reference case. The radiologist accepts
the estimate, corrects it, or corrects a region. The report is always theirs.

None of that exists yet, and the explanation layer has its own way to fail:
it can look convincing and point at the wrong bone. That has to be measured
against what a radiologist would point at, not assumed.

### Recipe first

Entry 0005 ends with a habit worth keeping: before building alone, find out
what already works. For bone age, a recent model called Deeplasia (Rassmann et
al., *Pediatric Radiology*, 2024) reports a mean absolute difference of 3.87
months on the public RSNA Pediatric Bone Age test set, with its code
published. So step one is to reproduce that number in my hands: an ensemble of
EfficientNets reading the masked hand at 512 and 1024 pixels, with the child's
sex as an input. Until that number comes out, there is no baseline to improve
on, only a guess.

The licences were written down before the first training run, not after.
Both the RSNA dataset and Deeplasia's code are for non-commercial research
use. That decides what the reproduction is for: it is a ruler and a sparring
partner, and nothing trained from it ships anywhere.

### The one exception to never flipping

The first rule of this repository is that images are never mirrored. On a
chest radiograph a flip moves the heart to the wrong side; on a knee MRI it
swaps medial for lateral, and half of Segond's labels named a side. The model
would learn the wrong anatomy and the metric would not notice.

Bone age is the first task where that argument does not hold. The label is a
single number for the whole hand, and mirroring the hand does not make its
skeleton any older or younger. So Greulich gets a narrow, recorded exception:
horizontal flip is allowed in the training transform, defined in exactly one
place in the code, and nowhere else — not in evaluation, not in inference, not
in test-time augmentation. A guard test checks both halves of that sentence.
The rule did not get weaker; it got a reason written next to it.

The cover above came out of the same theme by accident. Of the 571 fonts in
`pyfiglet`, exactly one draws GREULICH at the 48-by-4 box every cover has to
hit, and it is called `flipped` — it renders the name upside down. The cover
uses the letters spaced out in `eftitalic` instead, which fits the same box
and can be read the right way up.

## Numbers

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Greulich, trained result | **None.** Nothing has been trained yet |
| Target to reproduce | MAD 3.87 months on the RSNA test set (Deeplasia, as published) |
| Data | RSNA Pediatric Bone Age: 12,611 train, 1,425 validation, 200 test |
| Ruler built | MAD, RMSE, mean bias, MAD by sex and age band; no-image baseline (median bone age by sex, from train only) |
| Budget | Kaggle free GPU quota, three-week ceiling |

The 200 test images get opened once. Before that happens, a pre-registration
fixes the window the reproduction has to land in. My working proposal is 4.4
months or better, roughly two standard errors above the published figure for a
test set that small; the pre-registration is where that number becomes binding.
If it misses, the debugging happens on validation, and the test set stays
closed.

## What I got wrong

Nothing has run, so nothing has gone wrong yet. What the planned numbers will
not tell us is worth writing down now, before they arrive and look
authoritative.

The RSNA dataset does not expose patient identity. The project's second rule
is to split by patient, because the same child is X-rayed again and again
while their growth is followed. Here that rule cannot be enforced: the
official splits are by image, and the reproduction has to use them to be
comparable. Hashing images across splits catches exact duplicates and nothing
more. The limitation is declared rather than fixed, and any number from this
reproduction carries it.

The reference age is itself a reading. The 200 test labels are averages of
six radiologists, so a mean absolute difference measures agreement with
readers, not distance from biological truth. A model cannot meaningfully beat
the disagreement between the people who made its labels, and a number below
that disagreement should be treated as a leak until proven otherwise.
